What is the Debt Snowball and Emergency Fund Calculator?

This calculator simulates paying off several debts at once using the debt snowball method — attack the smallest balance first while paying minimums on everything else, then roll each freed-up minimum payment into the next-smallest balance — alongside building a starter and then full emergency fund. It turns a multi-debt household budget into a single month-by-month timeline.

How does the underlying math work?

Each month, every open debt accrues interest at its own APR. The calculator pays the minimum on every debt except the current smallest-balance target, which receives its minimum plus all of your disposable income (income minus living expenses minus every debt's minimum payment). The moment a debt reaches zero, its minimum payment is added to the pool attacking the next-smallest balance — the "snowball" rolling forward. Any one-time gift first tops up a $1,000 starter emergency fund, then goes straight onto the smallest balance. Once every debt is cleared, the full amount that used to service debt redirects to the emergency fund until it reaches your goal.

Real-world practical application

Households juggling a credit card, a 401(k) loan and a car loan at the same time use this to get a single, concrete order of operations instead of splitting extra payments evenly across everything (which usually takes longer and doesn't change behavior). It's also useful for testing how a tax refund, bonus, or gift changes the payoff date, and for seeing how soon a real safety net exists after the debt is gone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why pay off the smallest balance first instead of the highest interest rate?

The debt snowball method (smallest balance first) usually costs a bit more in total interest than the debt avalanche method (highest rate first), but it produces a payoff win faster, which research on behavior change shows helps many people stick with the plan. Either method beats paying only minimums.

Why build a starter emergency fund before attacking debt at all?

A small starter cushion, commonly $1,000, exists so an unexpected car repair or medical bill doesn't force you back onto a credit card while you're trying to pay one off. This calculator sets that cushion aside first, before any extra money goes toward the snowball.

What happens to a debt's minimum payment once it's paid off?

It rolls forward. The dollars that used to cover that debt's minimum payment get added to the amount attacking the next-smallest balance, so your total monthly debt payment stays the same throughout — only where it's pointed changes. That's the "snowball" effect.
