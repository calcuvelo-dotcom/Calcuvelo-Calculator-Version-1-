What is the Mortgage Calculator?

A mortgage payment looks like one number on a lender’s quote, but it is really three or four numbers added together: the principal and interest on the loan, a monthly share of your property tax, a monthly share of homeowners insurance, and any HOA dues. This calculator builds all of them from the home price, down payment, rate and term, and then shows what the loan will cost over its life.

It is aimed at the stage between “browsing listings” and “talking to a lender,” when you want to know whether a $400,000 house means a $2,000 payment or a $2,800 one. (If your real question is whether you can afford it, the Mortgage Affordability Calculator checks the payment against your income.)

Filling it in

  • Home price and down payment %. Putting down less than 20% usually adds private mortgage insurance (PMI), which this calculator does not model; budget roughly 0.3–1.5% of the loan per year on top.
  • Interest rate and term. Use the rate from an actual quote, not an advertised “as low as” rate, which often assumes excellent credit and discount points. Shorter terms normally carry lower rates, so compare 15- and 30-year quotes separately.
  • Property tax %. Your county’s effective rate, often between 0.5% and 2.5% of value a year. The listing or the county assessor’s site will have the last bill.
  • Insurance and HOA. Annual premium and monthly dues. Insurance quotes vary enormously by region, so get a real number if you can.

How does the underlying math work?

The principal-and-interest payment comes from the standard amortization formula, where L is the loan amount, i the monthly rate (annual ÷ 12) and n the number of monthly payments:

M = L × i × (1 + i)n ÷ [ (1 + i)n − 1 ]

Each month the lender charges interest on whatever you still owe, and the rest of the payment reduces the balance. Because the balance is highest at the start, interest takes the biggest bite early. The year-by-year table makes that visible: principal paid creeps upward every year while interest shrinks.

Worked example: a $400,000 home, two ways

Scenario 1 — 30 years. 20% down ($80,000) leaves a $320,000 loan. At 6.5% the principal-and-interest payment is $2,022.62. Add property tax at 1.1% ($367 a month) and $1,800 a year of insurance ($150 a month), and the all-in payment is about $2,539. Over 30 years you will pay roughly $408,142 in interest — more than the amount you borrowed.

Look at month one: interest is $320,000 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = $1,733, so only about $289 of that first $2,022 payment reduces what you owe.

Scenario 2 — 15 years. Same house and down payment, but a 15-year loan at 6.0% (an assumed, typically lower rate). Principal and interest rise to $2,700.34, the all-in payment to about $3,217 — but total interest falls to roughly $166,062.

The trade: about $678 more per month buys roughly $242,000 less interest and a mortgage-free home 15 years sooner. Whether that is a good deal depends on whether you can carry the higher payment comfortably and what else that $678 could do.

Real-world practical application

Beyond estimating a payment, the calculator is useful for three decisions. First, comparing offers: a quarter-point difference in rate on a $320,000 loan is worth around $50 a month. Second, testing the down payment: slide it from 10% to 20% and watch both the payment and the lifetime interest drop. Third, planning an early payoff: the amortization table shows exactly how much principal you will have cleared by any given year, which helps when deciding between extra payments and investing.

Limits worth knowing

The figures assume a fixed rate for the whole term, so an adjustable-rate loan will not match. Property tax and insurance are held constant, although in reality both tend to rise, so your all-in payment will likely creep up even though the principal-and-interest portion is fixed. Closing costs, moving costs and maintenance (a common rule of thumb is 1% of the home’s value a year) are not included but belong in your budget.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is so little of my early payment going to principal?

Interest is charged on the remaining balance, and the balance is largest at the start. On the $320,000 loan above, interest consumes about 86% of the first payment. The split gradually flips over the life of the loan, with the crossover point around year 19 for a 30-year mortgage at this rate.

Is a 15-year mortgage always better than a 30-year?

It saves a great deal of interest, but it demands a larger fixed payment and leaves less room for emergencies. Some borrowers take the 30-year loan for flexibility and make extra principal payments when they can, which captures part of the savings without the obligation.

Does this include PMI?

No. If you put down less than 20% on a conventional loan, expect to pay PMI until you reach about 20% equity. Add a rough monthly estimate to the all-in figure shown here.

Should my payment include property tax and insurance?

For budgeting, yes. Lenders usually collect them through escrow, so your actual monthly bill will include them, and the all-in figure is the one to compare with your income.

How much does one extra payment a year really save?

Quite a lot on a long loan. On the $320,000 example, one additional principal-only payment a year shortens the loan by about five and a half years and saves roughly $89,000 in interest, because every extra dollar stops accruing interest for the remaining decades.
