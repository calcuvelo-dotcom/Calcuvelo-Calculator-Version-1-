What is the Debt Payoff Calculator?

Credit card statements print a “minimum payment” and, in small type, a warning about how long that minimum would take. This calculator does that arithmetic for any payment you choose: enter the balance, the APR and what you plan to pay each month, and it returns the number of months to zero, the total interest and the grand total you will have paid.

The optional extra-payment box is the real point. It lets you see, in dollars and months, what a modest increase does to a balance that otherwise feels permanent.

How to use it

  1. Balance and APR. Copy both from your latest statement. Cards often quote a few different APRs; use the one that applies to purchases or the one on the balance you are paying down.
  2. Monthly payment. Enter what you can truly pay every month, not a one-time burst.
  3. Extra payment. Optional. Try $25, $50, $100 and see how the payoff curve moves; the second line on the chart is the accelerated plan.

If the payment you enter is no larger than a month’s interest, the calculator tells you so instead of showing a number, because the balance would never fall.

How does the underlying math work?

Each month the card adds one-twelfth of the APR to the balance, then your payment comes off. With a balance B, monthly rate i (APR ÷ 12) and fixed payment P, the number of months to reach zero is:

n = − ln( 1 − (i × B) ÷ P ) ÷ ln( 1 + i )

The logarithm exists only when P is larger than i × B, the first month’s interest — the same condition as the warning above. The calculator also runs a month-by-month simulation so the table and the totals match how statements round to the cent.

Worked example: $8,000 at 21.9% APR

The first month’s interest on $8,000 at 21.9% is $8,000 × 0.219 ÷ 12 = $146. Compare three payments:

  • $160 a month barely clears the interest. It takes about 135 months — over 11 years — and roughly $13,550 in interest, far more than the original balance.
  • $250 a month takes 49 months and costs about $4,125 in interest ($12,125 total).
  • $350 a month (the $250 plus $100 extra) clears the debt in about 30 months and saves roughly $1,677 in interest and 19 months compared with the $250 plan.

The first $100 per month beyond the interest-only level does vastly more good than the next $100, because the balance shrinks quickly and so does the interest charged on it.

Real-world practical application

People most often use this when deciding how aggressive to be with a tax refund or bonus, or when weighing a balance transfer: put the current plan in, note the interest total, and compare it with the cost of a transfer fee. It is also a sobering way to audit a “minimum payment only” habit. Many minimums are set near 1% of the balance plus interest, which stretches payoff across decades.

Assumptions to keep in mind

The calculator assumes no new purchases, a constant APR and a payment made on schedule every month. Variable-rate cards can change the APR under you, and a missed payment can trigger penalty rates. If you carry several balances, use this tool for each one and then plan the order with the Debt Snowball Calculator.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my balance barely drop when I pay on a high-APR card?

Because most of the payment is interest. At 21.9% on $8,000, about $146 of the first $250 payment is interest and only about $104 reduces the balance. The principal share grows slowly, which is why paying a little more has an outsized effect.

How much does paying twice a month help?

Slightly. Cards compute interest on a daily balance, so paying half the amount mid-cycle lowers the average balance a little. The effect is small compared with simply raising the total you pay each month.

Should I pay off the card or invest the money?

With a 20%+ APR, paying the card is nearly always the better return, since it is a guaranteed saving that very few investments can match. At low rates, the question is closer; the Payoff vs. Interest Calculator compares the two.

Does a 0% balance transfer beat this plan?

It can, if you clear the balance before the promo ends and the transfer fee (typically 3–5%) is smaller than the interest you would otherwise pay. The Debt Consolidation Calculator runs that comparison.

What if I have more than one card?

Enter each card separately for its timeline, then decide an order. The snowball-versus-avalanche guide walks through both methods with real numbers.
