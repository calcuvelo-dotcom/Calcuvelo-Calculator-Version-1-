What is the Retirement / 401(k) Calculator?

This calculator takes the unglamorous inputs of a workplace retirement plan — how old you are, what you earn, what you contribute, what your employer adds — and projects a balance at your retirement age. It then translates that balance into a rough monthly income so you can judge whether the destination looks like what you had in mind.

It is designed for the question most 401(k) holders actually have: “Am I on track, and what happens if I raise my contribution a few points?”

What to enter in each box

  • Current and retirement age. Retirement age sets the number of years of contributions and growth. Each extra working year does double duty: another year of contributions and one fewer year of withdrawals.
  • Current balance and salary. Use today’s plan balance and your gross annual pay.
  • Your contribution % and employer match %. The match is the percentage of your salary your employer adds, not the match formula itself. If your plan matches 50% of the first 6% you contribute, the employer adds 3% of salary, so enter 3.
  • Expected return. Seven percent is a commonly used long-run stock-heavy assumption before inflation; a conservative planner may use 5–6%.
  • Annual raise. Contributions are a percentage of salary, so raises grow them automatically. Two to three percent is a modest assumption.
  • Withdrawal rate. The share of the balance you plan to draw down in the first year of retirement; 4% is the traditional rule of thumb.

How does the underlying math work?

Each month the balance grows by one-twelfth of the annual return, and a contribution equal to (your % + employer %) of one-twelfth of your salary is added. Once a year the salary is increased by the raise percentage.

balancem = balancem−1 × (1 + r÷12) + salary ÷ 12 × (your % + match %)

The monthly retirement income estimate is simply the final balance × withdrawal rate ÷ 12. It is a planning shortcut, not a guarantee: it ignores taxes on withdrawals, Social Security and the order of market returns.

Worked example: a 35-year-old, 30 years to go

Take someone aged 35 with $40,000 in the plan, an $85,000 salary, contributing 6% with a 3% employer match, expecting 7% a year and 2% annual raises, retiring at 65.

  • Projected balance at 65: about $1,277,275.
  • Total contributed from now on: about $310,346; investment growth supplies the other roughly $926,929.
  • At a 4% withdrawal rate: about $4,258 a month.

Now raise the personal contribution from 6% to 10%, which costs about $3,400 more in the first year. The projection rises to roughly $1,700,659, or about $5,669 a month — around $423,000 more at retirement for an extra 4% of pay. Contributions rose by about $138,000 over the 30 years; growth did the rest.

One honest caveat: those are nominal dollars. At 3% inflation, $1,277,275 in 30 years is worth roughly $526,000 in today’s purchasing power. Compare any result with what you spend today, in today’s dollars.

Real-world practical application

The most common use is the “one more percent” test: change the contribution box by a point and see the retirement balance move. The second is the match audit — if you are contributing less than the percentage that earns your full employer match, you are leaving part of your compensation on the table, and the calculator shows how large that gap becomes over decades. A third use is retirement-age planning: compare 62, 65 and 67 to see how much the extra years are worth.

How to read the result sensibly

Treat the output as a range finder, not a forecast. Run it three times — at 5%, 7% and 9% — and plan around the low case. Remember that the 4% withdrawal rule comes from historical analysis of 30-year retirements; a longer retirement, or a very large share of your income coming from this account, argues for a lower rate. Annual contribution limits, catch-up rules for those over 50, and Roth versus pre-tax treatment change from year to year, so check the current IRS figures rather than relying on any article’s numbers.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much of my salary should I be saving for retirement?

A commonly cited target is 10–15% of gross income including any employer match, starting in your twenties or thirties. If you start later, the required percentage climbs. Use the calculator to test your own age and balance rather than relying on a single rule.

Is a 7% return realistic?

It is a reasonable long-run average for a stock-heavy portfolio before inflation, but individual decades vary widely and some are far worse. Many planners use 5–6% for a more cautious projection. The honest answer is to run several rates and see how much the outcome changes.

What does “vesting” mean for my employer match?

Vesting is the schedule on which employer contributions become fully yours. Your own contributions are always yours; the match may be forfeited in part if you leave before you are fully vested, so check your plan documents before a job change.

Pre-tax or Roth 401(k)?

Pre-tax contributions lower taxable income now and are taxed on withdrawal; Roth contributions are taxed now and grow tax-free. Which wins depends mostly on whether your tax rate will be higher now or in retirement. This calculator projects the balance and does not model either tax treatment.

Does the projection include Social Security?

No. Social Security, a pension or part-time work would be on top of the monthly figure shown, which makes this tool a conservative picture of the 401(k) alone.
