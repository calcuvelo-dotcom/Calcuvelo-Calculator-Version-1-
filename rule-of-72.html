What is the Rule of 72 Calculator?

Here is a trick bankers have used for centuries: divide 72 by an annual growth rate and you get, roughly, the number of years it takes something to double. Earn 8% and you double in about nine years; inflate at 4% and prices double in about eighteen. This calculator runs that shortcut next to the exact answer, so you can see how good the approximation is and how much a given rate compounds over a longer horizon.

It is as much a teaching aid as a calculator: it lets you build the intuition that growth rates are not “small numbers” when they run for decades.

Using it

  • Growth rate. An investment return, an inflation rate, an economy’s GDP growth, a population growth rate, a rate of interest on debt — anything that compounds.
  • Rule number. Defaults to 72. Try 69.3 (the continuous-compounding version) or 70 (a common alternative) to see how they compare with the exact answer.
  • Starting value and horizon. Optional context: see what an amount grows to over a chosen number of years, and how many doublings that represents.

How does the underlying math work?

The exact doubling time solves (1 + r)t = 2 for t:

t = ln 2 ÷ ln(1 + r)

For small r, ln(1 + r) is close to r, and ln 2 is about 0.693, so t ≈ 69.3 ÷ (rate in percent). The number 72 is a convenient nudge upward: it divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12, which makes mental arithmetic easy, and it happens to be the most accurate for rates around 8%. At very low rates 70 is closer; at very high rates, the rule loses precision.

Worked example: how good is the shortcut?

RateRule of 72Exact
2%36 years35.0 years
6%12 years11.9 years
8%9 years9.01 years
12%6 years6.12 years

At 8%, $10,000 over 30 years becomes about $100,627 — roughly three and a third doublings (30 ÷ 9.01), a tenfold increase. At 6% the same 30 years yields about $57,435, little more than five times the start. Two points of return, in other words, turn a 5.7× outcome into a 10× one.

Real-world practical application

The rule is best used to sanity-check claims and plans. A pitch promising 12% a year implies money doubling in six years — ask yourself whether that is credible. Run it backwards: to double in ten years you need about 7.2% a year. Apply it to fees: moving from a 6% net return to a 7% one shortens the doubling time from 12 years to about 10.3, so a one-point fee costs you more than a year and a half per doubling. And apply it to cost of living: at 3% inflation, prices double in about 24 years, which is why a retirement plan must outpace it.

Where it breaks down

The Rule of 72 assumes a single steady compounding rate and no deposits or withdrawals, so it does not handle regular contributions. It becomes less accurate above about 20% (an interest rate on a payday-style loan, for example), where the exact formula should be used instead. It also says nothing about risk: a rate of 8% is a long-run average, not a promise that your money doubles on schedule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why 72 and not 69 or 70?

The exact figure for small rates is about 69.3. Seventy-two is slightly higher but divides cleanly into many common rates, and it is more accurate for rates in the 6–10% range most investors care about. Seventy is a middle-ground alternative.

Does the rule work for money I owe?

Yes, in reverse: debt at 18% APR left alone doubles in about four years. That is a quick way to feel how dangerous a high-interest balance becomes.

Can I use it for GDP or population growth?

Yes. An economy growing 3% a year doubles in roughly 24 years; a population growing 1% takes about 72. The math is the same for anything that compounds.

How do I find the rate needed to double in a given number of years?

Divide 72 by the number of years. To double in 12 years you need about 6%; in 8 years, about 9%. The exact figure is 21/years − 1.

Does it work with monthly contributions?

No. It describes a single lump sum compounding. For a plan with regular deposits, use the Compound Interest Calculator.
