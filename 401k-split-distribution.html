What is the Roth 401(k) Split Distribution Stress Relief Calculator?

When you leave an employer, you can split an old 401(k): roll most of it tax-free into a new plan or IRA, and cash out a portion to cover an immediate need. This calculator figures out exactly how much of the cashed-out portion survives taxes and penalties, and translates that net cash — combined with whatever savings you already have — into how many months of expenses it actually covers.

How does the underlying math work?

Only the cashed-out distribution is a taxable event: the calculator reduces it by your combined federal-plus-state tax-rate estimate, an early-withdrawal penalty if you're under 59½ (10% by default), and a flat plan processing fee. The amount rolled directly into a new 401(k) keeps its tax-deferred status, so it is reduced only by the flat fee, never by tax or penalty. The resulting net distribution is added to your existing emergency fund and checking/savings balances to form a total cushion, which is then divided by your total monthly expenses to get a runway in months — a standard way to measure how long cash on hand would last if nothing else changed.

Real-world practical application

This is built for the specific, stressful moment of a job change: you need some cash now — to cover a gap between paychecks, a medical bill, or moving costs — but don't want to sacrifice your whole retirement balance to taxes and penalties by cashing out everything. Running the numbers before deciding how much to split off shows whether a smaller distribution still buys enough breathing room, so you can roll over more and keep it growing tax-deferred.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the rolled-over portion not taxed, but the cashed-out portion is?

A rollover moves money directly between tax-deferred retirement accounts, so the IRS doesn't treat it as income. Cashing out a portion instead — taking a distribution — is treated as ordinary income in the year you receive it, and if you're under 59½ it typically adds a 10% early-withdrawal penalty on top.

How much should I assume gets withheld for taxes?

Plans are generally required to withhold 20% for federal taxes on a cashed-out distribution, and your state may withhold more. The withholding is just an estimate toward what you'll actually owe — your real tax bill depends on your total income and bracket for the year, so set the tax-rate input to your best estimate of your combined marginal rate.

What counts toward my "runway" in this calculator?

Runway is the net cash from the distribution plus any emergency fund and checking/savings balance you enter, divided by your total monthly expenses. It estimates how many months you could cover your bills from cash on hand alone, a standard way financial planners gauge how much cushion a household has.
