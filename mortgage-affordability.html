What is the Mortgage Affordability Calculator?

Most mortgage tools start from the house and ask what the payment is. This one starts from you. You enter your income, your existing monthly debts and your savings, plus a home you are considering, and it checks three things lenders care about: whether the housing payment is a reasonable share of your income, whether all your debts together are, and whether you can fund the down payment and closing costs without emptying your accounts.

The answer is a plain verdict — comfortable, borderline or not yet — along with the reason, so you know which lever to pull.

Entering your numbers

  • Annual household income. Gross (before tax), all borrowers on the loan combined.
  • Other monthly debt payments. Car loans, student loans, minimum card payments and any other recurring debt. Do not include rent you will stop paying or utilities.
  • Available savings. Cash you are willing to commit to the purchase, not your emergency fund.
  • Price, down payment %, rate, years. Use a real rate quote. Property tax and insurance default to typical figures; replace them with local ones.
  • Closing costs. The tool uses 3% of the price, a middle estimate; lenders, title fees and prepaid taxes typically fall between 2% and 5%.

How does the underlying math work?

First the monthly principal-and-interest payment comes from the standard amortization formula. Property tax, insurance and any HOA dues are added to get the total housing cost. Then two ratios, both against gross monthly income, are computed:

front-end ratio = housing cost ÷ gross monthly income
back-end ratio (DTI) = (housing cost + other monthly debt) ÷ gross monthly income

The traditional benchmarks are 28% for the front-end ratio and 36% for the back-end ratio, with many conventional loans allowed up to roughly 43% and some programs more. The verdict is “comfortable” when all three tests pass (both ratios within 28/36 and cash to close covered), “borderline” when the back-end ratio is within 43% and cash is covered, and “not yet” otherwise.

Worked example: $160,000 of income, two homes

Income $160,000 (monthly $13,333), $715 a month in other debt, savings of $70,000, a 6.5% 30-year loan, 1.1% property tax and $2,000 of insurance.

A $650,000 home, 20% down. The loan is $520,000, principal and interest about $3,287, total housing about $4,049. That is 30.4% of income (front-end) and 35.7% with the other debt (back-end). The back-end ratio squeaks under 36%, but the front-end ratio breaks the 28% line, and cash to close is about $149,500 against $70,000 saved. Verdict: not yet.

A $450,000 home, 20% down, savings of $120,000. Principal and interest are about $2,275, total housing about $2,855: front-end 21.4%, back-end 26.8%. Cash needed is about $103,500, which $120,000 covers. Verdict: comfortably affordable.

The gap between the two answers is the whole point: the same income supports a $450,000 purchase far more easily than a $650,000 one.

Real-world practical application

Use it to find your ceiling by trial: adjust the price until the verdict flips. Then change one thing at a time to see what moves the result. Paying off a $400-a-month car loan reduces the back-end ratio by 3 points; a larger down payment lowers the payment and the ratios together but raises cash needed; a higher rate or longer horizon changes the monthly cost sharply. Running three or four versions in ten minutes is far more instructive than any single estimate.

Limits worth knowing

A lender will look at your credit score, employment history, assets and the specific loan program, none of which this tool sees. Rates differ by down payment and score, mortgage insurance is not included for down payments below 20%, and maintenance, utilities and moving costs sit outside both ratios yet belong in your budget. The 28/36 pair is a conservative convention; the right limit for you depends on your other priorities and how stable your income is.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do lenders really use 28% and 36%?

They are long-standing guidelines. Many conventional lenders will go higher, often to a back-end DTI of 43% or more with strong credit and reserves, and government-backed programs allow higher still. Treat 28/36 as a comfort zone, not a hard stop.

Should I use gross or take-home income?

Lenders use gross, so the calculator does too. But when budgeting for yourself, check the payment against your actual take-home pay; a payment that is 28% of gross can be 35% or more of what lands in your account.

Why does the tool check savings as well as income?

Because many buyers are approved on income and then run short at closing. Cash needed is the down payment plus closing costs, and spending the very last dollar leaves nothing for repairs or a bad month.

What are the biggest levers for improving the verdict?

Lower other debt, increase the down payment, pick a lower-priced home, or find a lower rate. The fastest improvement per dollar is often paying off a high-payment debt, such as a car loan.

Does this replace a mortgage pre-approval?

No. It is a self-check before you apply. A pre-approval verifies your income and credit and is what sellers expect to see with an offer.
