What is the Present & Future Value Calculator?

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year, because today’s dollar can be put to work. This calculator turns that idea into two practical questions. Future value: if I invest this much now (and perhaps add a regular payment), what will it become? Present value: if I need a specific amount at a future date, how much must I have now to get there?

It is the general-purpose engine behind most savings goals, tuition plans and valuation questions, which is why the controls are deliberately simple.

Using the two modes

  • Grow a lump sum → FV. Enter a starting amount, an optional payment each period, the annual rate, the number of periods and the compounding frequency (monthly, quarterly or annually). The result is the value at the end.
  • Discount a future sum → PV. Enter the future target, an optional payment each period, the rate and the periods. The result is the amount you need to have today. Adding a payment lowers it, since the payments do part of the work.

The “periods” box counts in compounding periods: for 10 years of monthly compounding, enter 120; for 10 years compounded annually, enter 10.

How does the underlying math work?

With a periodic rate i (annual rate ÷ periods per year), n periods and a payment c at the end of each period:

FV = PV × (1 + i)n + c × [ (1 + i)n − 1 ] ÷ i
PV = [ FV − c × ((1 + i)n − 1) ÷ i ] ÷ (1 + i)n

Present value is just the future-value equation rearranged: divide a future amount by the growth factor to bring it back to today. The rate used in that division is called the discount rate, and choosing it is the real judgment call.

Worked example: one of each

Future value. You have $15,000 and add $200 a month for 10 years at 6%, compounded monthly (120 periods). The result is about $60,067. You put in $39,000 ($15,000 + 120 × $200); the other roughly $21,067 is growth.

Present value. You want $50,000 in 10 years and expect 5% a year, compounded annually (10 periods, no payments). You need about $30,696 today. Now add $1,000 a year of contributions in the payment box: the present value needed falls to about $22,974, because the payments supply about $12,578 of the $50,000 target by themselves.

Same goal, different mixes of “money now” and “money along the way” — the calculator lets you see the trade-off directly.

Real-world practical application

Present value is the quiet workhorse of financial decisions. It shows up when comparing a lump-sum settlement with a stream of payments (discount the stream and compare), when valuing a promised future payout such as a bonus or buyout, when setting aside money for a known future bill like a tuition payment, and when a business estimates what a future cash flow is worth today. The method is the same each time: pick a sensible discount rate, then bring everything to the same date.

Reading the result carefully

The result is only as good as the rate. A high discount rate makes future money look small; a low one makes it look large. For a savings goal, use the return you realistically expect on the money; for a settlement offer, use a rate that reflects your alternatives and the risk of the payer. Also note that payments here are assumed to occur at the end of each period (an ordinary annuity); payments at the start of each period would be slightly more valuable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is present value always smaller than future value?

Because money you hold today can earn a return, so a given future amount needs less than that amount today. The higher the rate and the longer the wait, the bigger the gap.

What discount rate should I use?

For a personal goal, the rate you expect to earn on a comparable-risk investment. For comparing a settlement, a rate near what you could safely earn elsewhere. Run the calculation at two or three rates to see how sensitive the answer is.

What is the difference between an ordinary annuity and an annuity due?

An ordinary annuity pays at the end of each period; an annuity due pays at the start. This calculator uses the ordinary form. Moving every payment one period earlier would multiply the annuity part by (1 + i).

How do I compare a lump sum with monthly payments?

Pick a discount rate, bring each payment back to today’s value, and add them up. Compare that total with the lump sum on offer; if the lump sum is larger, it is the better deal at that rate.

Does compounding frequency matter?

A little. Moving from annual to monthly compounding at 6% raises the effective rate from 6.00% to about 6.17%. Over long periods the gap grows, but the rate and the number of years dominate.
