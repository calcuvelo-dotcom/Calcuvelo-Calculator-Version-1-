      What is the GIC & Cash Ladder Yield Optimizer?

      Canadians with cash to park usually face the same fork: leave it in a high-interest savings account (HISA) where it stays available but the rate can fall at any time, or lock some of it in guaranteed investment certificates (GICs) that pay a fixed rate for a fixed term. A GIC ladder splits the difference by buying GICs of different lengths so something matures every year. This tool projects both approaches side by side, after tax, for your amount and time horizon, and tells you how much the ladder gains and what you give up in access to the money.

      It is built for emergency funds that are larger than they need to be, house-down-payment savings with a known date, and anyone holding TFSA or RRSP cash.

      Setting it up

      • Starting amount and monthly addition. What you have now and what you plan to add each month. Monthly additions are swept into GICs once a year for balanced and maximum-yield ladders.
      • Time horizon (1–5 years). When you expect to need the money. GICs longer than your horizon are never bought.
      • Account type. TFSA and RRSP grow tax-free during accumulation in this model; a non-registered account taxes the interest at the marginal rate you enter.
      • Liquidity preference. Fully liquid keeps 40% in the savings account and ladders the rest across short GICs. Balanced keeps 10% as a buffer and spreads the rest across one-year to horizon-length GICs. Maximum yield puts everything into the longest GICs.

      How does the underlying math work?

      The savings account is compounded monthly. It earns a promotional new-money rate for the first five months, then reverts to a base rate. Each GIC compounds annually at the rate for its term, and when a GIC matures, the proceeds are reinvested for the remaining time. The ending value of a single GIC is:

      ending value = principal × (1 + term rate)years

      For non-registered money, the interest earned is reduced by your marginal tax rate. The effective after-tax yield shown for each strategy is the single annual rate that would grow your deposit schedule to the projected after-tax ending value, which makes the two strategies comparable even though their cash flows differ. The benchmark rates are listed in the assumptions box above, dated September 2026.

      Worked example: $25,000 for three years

      Enter $25,000, no monthly addition, a 3-year horizon and a TFSA, using the benchmark rates on this page.

      • Savings account only: about $27,351 at the end, an effective yield of about 3.00% (the promotional rate fades after five months).
      • Balanced ladder (10% buffer plus 1-, 2- and 3-year GICs): about $27,786, about 3.53% — roughly $435 more.
      • Fully liquid ladder: about $27,563 (3.26%), $212 ahead. Maximum yield: about $27,866 (3.62%), $514 ahead.

      Switch to a non-registered account at a 30% marginal rate and everything shrinks: the savings account ends near $26,646 (2.13%) and the balanced ladder near $26,950 (2.51%), a gap of about $304. Tax takes 30% of the interest in both cases, so the ladder’s advantage survives, but its dollar value falls.

      Real-world practical application

      The clearest use is a house fund with a fixed date. If you will need $60,000 in three years, you can buy GICs that mature just before that date rather than gamble on a savings rate that may drop. Another is an emergency fund that has grown beyond six months of expenses: keep a few months in a savings account and ladder the excess. For retirees or near-retirees holding cash in a TFSA or RRSP, the ladder provides a predictable schedule of maturities that can be spent or reinvested as rates change.

      Reading the result and its limits

      The “more after tax” figure is a projection from benchmark rates, not an offer: actual rates vary by institution, change daily, and your savings account rate may move in either direction. Savings rates are variable, so the savings-account side is the less certain of the two. GICs are insured by CDIC up to $100,000 per depositor, per insured category, per member institution, so a large ladder may need to be spread across institutions or account types; the tool flags balances that exceed this. Rates on this page are as of September 2026 — confirm current rates before acting, and note that registered-account contribution limits change every year.

      Frequently Asked Questions

      Is my money safe in a GIC ladder?

      Deposits at CDIC member institutions are insured up to $100,000 per depositor, per insured category, per institution. Splitting a ladder across institutions or across account types (for example TFSA, RRSP and non-registered) can extend coverage. Confirm that an institution is a CDIC member before you deposit, and note that credit unions are covered by provincial schemes with different rules.

      What happens if I need the money before a GIC matures?

      Most non-redeemable GICs cannot be cashed early. Cashable or redeemable GICs usually pay a lower rate. That is why a ladder keeps a savings-account buffer and staggers maturities, so that some money becomes free every year without breaking a term.

      Why does a TFSA or RRSP change the result so much?

      Inside a TFSA or RRSP, interest is not taxed as it compounds, so the whole posted rate works for you. In a non-registered account, interest is added to your income each year and taxed at your marginal rate, which cuts the effective yield by roughly the amount of your tax rate.

      How often should I rebuild the ladder?

      When a GIC matures, decide on the spot: reinvest it at the long end of the ladder to keep the schedule going, or move the cash into the savings account if rates favor it or you need the money. Many people review the ladder once a year, when the shortest rung matures.

      Are these rates guaranteed?

      The GIC rates for a term you have already purchased are fixed. The rates on this page are benchmarks for illustration, dated September 2026; the savings-account rate is variable and promotions end. Always check the current rate with the institution.
