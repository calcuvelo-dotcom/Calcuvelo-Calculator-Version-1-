What is the Inflation Calculator?

The Inflation Calculator shows how much a fixed sum of money will really be able to buy in the future after a given number of years of price increases, and how much larger that sum would need to grow just to keep pace.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter the amount of money you want to track — for example a savings balance, a fixed pension, or a price.
  2. Enter the number of years into the future.
  3. Set an average annual inflation rate. Around 2–3% reflects long-run norms; try a higher figure to stress-test.
  4. Read the results. "Future purchasing power" is what today's amount will buy in future dollars. "Value lost" and "% lost" quantify the erosion. "Needed to keep pace" is how large the sum must grow just to stand still. The chart shows the decline year by year.

How does the underlying math work?

Inflation compounds, just like interest. The future purchasing power of a fixed amount is:

PurchasingPower = A ÷ (1 + f)ʸ

And the amount you would need in the future to buy what A buys today is:

AmountNeeded = A × (1 + f)ʸ
  • A — amount of money today
  • f — average annual inflation rate (as a decimal)
  • y — number of years

The share of value lost is 1 − 1 ÷ (1 + f)ʸ.

Real-world practical application

People use it to check whether a fixed pension or annuity payment will still cover expenses in twenty years, to translate a salary offer or savings goal into today's purchasing power, and to see why cash sitting in a low-interest account quietly loses value every single year.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a normal rate of inflation?

Many central banks, including the U.S. Federal Reserve, target about 2%. The long-run historical average in the United States is closer to 3%. Individual years vary widely, and categories like healthcare and tuition often rise faster than the overall index.

How is inflation measured?

The headline figure usually comes from the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which tracks the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services a typical household buys. The year-over-year percentage change in that index is the inflation rate.

How do I protect my savings from inflation?

Holding only cash locks in a slow loss of purchasing power. Over long periods a diversified mix of stocks and real assets has outpaced inflation, and TIPS and I-bonds are government bonds designed specifically to track the CPI.
