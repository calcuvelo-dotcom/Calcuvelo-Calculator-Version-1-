What is the Inflation Calculator?

Money has two values: the number printed on it and what it will buy. Inflation is the slow gap between the two. This calculator takes an amount, a number of years and an assumed inflation rate and shows both sides of that gap — what today’s sum will be worth in future purchasing power, and how large the future sum must be to buy what today’s buys.

It is a tool for retirement planners, savers sitting on cash, and anyone looking at a future salary, pension or settlement figure and wondering what it really means.

Choosing the inputs

  • Amount. A sum in today’s dollars: a savings balance, an annual budget, a fixed pension payment.
  • Years and months. The gap between now and the moment you care about.
  • Inflation rate. Central banks in many countries aim for around 2% a year; US consumer prices have averaged roughly 3% over the long run, but spiked above 9% in 2022. A three-scenario approach — 2%, 3%, 4% — is more honest than a single guess.

How does the underlying math work?

Prices compound just like interest does. If prices rise by f per year for t years, the two figures the calculator reports are:

purchasing power = amount ÷ (1 + f)t
amount needed to keep pace = amount × (1 + f)t

The two are mirror images: the first divides, the second multiplies by the same growth factor. The “value lost” figure is simply the amount minus its future purchasing power.

Worked example: $50,000 over 25 years

Suppose a fixed $50,000 is meant to cover a year of living costs, 25 years from now.

  • At 2% inflation it buys the equivalent of about $30,477 today; you would need about $82,030 to keep pace.
  • At 3% it buys about $23,880 (a loss of 52%); you would need about $104,689.
  • At 4% it buys about $18,756; you would need about $133,292.

The difference between 2% and 4% is not “twice as bad” — it is more than $50,000 of required future income, because the effect compounds. This is why a fixed-dollar pension with no cost-of-living adjustment is far weaker than its headline number suggests.

Real-world practical application

Three situations come up again and again. Retirement budgeting: convert today’s spending into the future figure you need to cover it. Salary negotiation: a 3% raise in a 3% inflation year is no raise at all in purchasing-power terms. Cash holding: money in an account paying less than inflation is shrinking in real terms even though the statement balance rises; the Real ROI Calculator measures that gap precisely.

A caution about averages

A single inflation rate is a simplification. Your own inflation depends on what you buy: housing, healthcare and education have often outpaced the headline index, while electronics and some goods have fallen in price. And inflation can swing sharply from year to year, so a steady-rate projection is best read as one scenario among several.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which inflation rate should I use for long-term planning?

Many planners use 2.5–3% as a base case and test 4% as a stress case. Using your local central bank’s target rate is reasonable for a mild scenario, but history says to leave room for higher.

Why does the calculator show two different numbers?

They answer opposite questions. Purchasing power asks “what will today’s money be worth then?” The amount needed asks “how much money then equals today’s buying power?” Both use the same compounding factor in opposite directions.

Does inflation help borrowers?

Yes, with fixed-rate debt: you repay with dollars that are worth less than the ones you borrowed. That benefit mostly goes to people with long fixed-rate loans, such as a 30-year mortgage, and does not apply to variable-rate debt that rises with prices.

Is my savings account rate enough to stay ahead of inflation?

Only if the rate is higher than inflation. Subtract the inflation rate from your rate: a 4% account with 3% inflation gains about 1% in real terms, before tax. After tax it may be zero or negative.

Can I use this for past inflation?

The compounding math is the same, but past inflation is better looked up from official price-index data, which varies year by year, rather than approximated with a steady average.
