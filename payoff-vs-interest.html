What is the Payoff vs. Interest Calculator?

You have a lump of cash and a car loan. Paying the loan off feels responsible; keeping the cash invested feels clever. Which one is right depends on one comparison — the loan’s interest rate against what the cash could earn elsewhere — and on being fair about what each choice does to your monthly budget. This calculator makes that comparison with equal cash flows on both sides, so neither option gets a hidden advantage.

It also shows when the loan would end if you simply kept paying, and whether the car is worth more than you owe.

What to enter

  • Loan balance, APR and monthly payment. Take them from your latest loan statement; the balance should be the payoff amount.
  • Car value. A market estimate; used only to flag negative equity (owing more than the car is worth).
  • Alternative annual return. The return you would realistically expect on the cash if you kept it: a savings rate, a bond yield, or an equity assumption. Be conservative — a stock return is not guaranteed, while the loan interest you avoid is.

How does the underlying math work?

The calculator first simulates the loan month by month to find how long it would run and how much interest it would charge. It then compares two ways of using the same resources over that period:

  • Keep the loan. You keep paying the monthly bill from your income and invest the lump sum today. It grows to B × (1 + j)n, where j is the monthly alternative return.
  • Pay it off. The loan disappears today, and each month you invest the payment you no longer owe. That stream grows to the sum of each payment compounded from the month it is invested.

When the alternative return equals the loan’s APR, the two come out exactly equal — the test that confirms the comparison is fair. Below the APR, paying off wins; above it, investing wins.

Worked example: two loans, two answers

A 7.5% loan. Balance $14,000, payment $420, so about 38 months remain and the loan would cost about $1,748 in interest. If you could earn 4.5% on the cash, keeping the loan and investing ends at about $16,140, while paying off and investing the freed-up $420 a month ends at about $16,907 — paying off wins by roughly $767. Only if the investment returned more than about 7.5% would keeping the loan come out ahead, and that return is anything but guaranteed.

A 4.9% loan. Balance $9,000, payment $300, about 33 months left, interest about $620. With a 7% alternative return, keeping the loan and investing ends about $302 ahead ($10,904 versus $10,603). That small edge depends on a 7% return arriving on schedule over a short window — a much weaker case than it looks, and a worse deal if the market falls.

Real-world practical application

The calculator is built for windfalls: a bonus, a tax refund, an inheritance. It frames the choice as a rate comparison, which also tells you where the money goes first if you have several debts: the highest APR first, the car loan only if its rate beats what your other options earn. It is also a good way to see how little a very low-rate loan costs, which is often a reason to leave it alone and keep your emergency cushion intact.

Cautions

Liquidity matters in a way the math does not show. Cash you use to pay off a loan is gone; cash you keep can cover a job loss or repair bill. Many people reasonably keep a three- to six-month cushion before prepaying anything. Also check the loan agreement for a prepayment penalty, which is rare but exists, and remember that the returns you assume on an investment are estimates, not promises.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is paying off a car loan early always smart?

It is a guaranteed return equal to the loan’s APR, which is attractive when the rate is high and less so when it is very low. It is a poor idea if it drains your emergency cushion.

What does “upside-down” mean?

You owe more than the car is worth. It does not change the payoff math, but it matters if you plan to sell or trade the car, because the sale will not cover the loan.

Why does the calculator invest the freed-up payment in the payoff case?

Because otherwise the comparison would be lopsided: one side keeps its cash invested while the other loses it. Paying off frees the monthly payment, and fair accounting gives that money somewhere to go.

How should I choose the alternative return?

Match it to where you would actually put the money. A savings account rate is a safe floor; an equity return is an expectation, not a guarantee. If investing wins only under the optimistic assumption, treat that as a tie and lean toward the sure thing.

Do early-payoff penalties exist on auto loans?

Rarely on modern loans, but some contracts include one or use precomputed interest. Read the payoff terms or ask the lender for a written payoff quote before sending any money.
