What is the Real ROI Calculator?

When a fund advertises “8% a year,” that figure is the starting line, not the finish. Tax takes a share of the gain, and inflation quietly reduces what the remaining dollars will buy. This calculator walks the headline return through both so you can see the number that actually matters: how much more you will be able to buy at the end than at the start.

It is built for investors comparing an account that is taxed with one that is not, and for anyone who suspects their “safe” return is not outrunning prices.

Inputs

  • Initial investment and years. The amount invested once, and the holding period.
  • Nominal annual return. The return before tax and inflation.
  • Inflation rate. Your assumption for the average yearly price rise over the period.
  • Capital-gains tax rate. The percentage of the gain paid in tax when you sell. Enter 0 for a tax-sheltered account such as a Roth IRA or a TFSA. Long-term US rates are 0%, 15% or 20% depending on income; check the current brackets.

How does the underlying math work?

There are three stages. First the investment grows to its nominal ending value, then tax is taken from the gain, and finally the result is deflated by inflation back to today’s dollars:

nominal = P × (1 + g)y
after tax = nominal − tax rate × (nominal − P)
real value = after tax ÷ (1 + inflation)y

The annualized real rate is found by taking the y-th root of (real value ÷ P) and subtracting one. Note it is not simply “return minus inflation”: the exact relationship is (1 + g) ÷ (1 + f) − 1, which is slightly lower than g − f.

Worked example: $20,000 at 8% for 15 years

Assume 3% inflation and a 15% capital-gains tax on the gain at sale.

  • Nominal ending value: about $63,443.
  • Tax on the $43,443 gain: about $6,517, leaving about $56,927 (7.22% a year after tax).
  • In today’s purchasing power: about $36,539, a real return of about 4.10% a year.

Put the same money in a tax-free account and the real value is about $40,722, or 4.85% a year — roughly $4,183 more, all from the wrapper. The headline 8% has shrunk to about half in real terms in the taxable case, which is why practitioners talk about “real” returns when planning.

Real-world practical application

Use it to settle a few recurring debates: is a 4% certificate of deposit actually positive after 3% inflation and tax on the interest? How much extra does a tax-advantaged account really deliver on the same investment? Is a fund’s 6.5% worth holding in a taxable account against a bond ladder? Entering the options side by side makes the answer visible in a single chart.

What this model simplifies

Tax is applied once, at sale, on the total gain. Funds that pay taxable dividends or distributions each year pay tax along the way, which lowers compounding a little more than this model shows. Interest income is taxed at ordinary income rates rather than capital-gains rates. Fees are not included: a 1% annual fund fee is a real cost that belongs in the return you enter. Treat the output as a good approximation for a buy-and-hold investment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why not just subtract inflation from the return?

It is close, but not exact. With an 8% return and 3% inflation, the simple subtraction gives 5.00%, while the exact figure is (1.08 ÷ 1.03) − 1 = 4.85%. The gap is small at low rates and grows as inflation rises.

Which tax rate should I enter?

Use the rate that applies to the kind of gain you expect: long-term capital gains for most stock and fund holdings held over a year, ordinary income rates for interest. Enter 0 if the money is in a Roth account, TFSA or similar. Rules change, so check the current ones.

What is a good real rate of return?

Long-run stock-market real returns have often been quoted around 5–7% before tax; bonds and cash have historically been much lower, sometimes near zero or below. The real return you need depends on your goal, not a benchmark.

Does this work for a savings account?

Yes. Enter the account’s rate as the nominal return and your marginal income tax rate as the tax rate. Many savings accounts have a real after-tax return near zero or slightly negative in higher-inflation periods.

Why does tax matter more over longer periods?

Because the gain, and therefore the tax, grows with the holding period. The dollar cost of tax on a 15-year investment is much larger than on a 3-year one, which is why tax-sheltered accounts matter most for long-term savings.
