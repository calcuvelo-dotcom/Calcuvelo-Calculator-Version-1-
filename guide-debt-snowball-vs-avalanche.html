Debt snowball vs. debt avalanche: which pays off faster?

Run your own numbers: Debt Snowball & Emergency Fund Calculator, Debt Payoff Calculator, Debt Consolidation Calculator.

Ask ten people how to pay off several debts and you will hear two confident answers. One camp says to attack the smallest balance first, collect the quick win, and let momentum carry you. The other says to attack the highest interest rate first, because that is where the money leaks fastest. Both camps have a name — the snowball and the avalanche — and both are certain they are right.

The useful question is not which method has the better slogan. It is how much the choice actually changes your timeline and your bill, and the honest answer depends on the debts in front of you. So let’s run the numbers on two sets of debts, one where the choice barely matters and one where it does.

The short answer

For the first set of debts below, both methods finish in exactly the same month (month 24) and the avalanche saves about $208 in interest. The snowball, in return, hands you your first cleared account in month 5 instead of month 12. For a second set, where one very large balance carries a very high rate, the avalanche saves about $1,050 and finishes a month sooner. Either method beats paying only the minimums by a mile.

The setup: three debts and $800 a month

Imagine someone with three debts and a plan to put $800 a month toward them — the $400 of minimum payments plus $400 extra.

DebtBalanceAPRMinimum
Visa$2,10017%$60
Store card$5,40027%$130
Personal loan$8,80011%$210

Total owed: $16,300. Before comparing strategies, a baseline: if this person paid only the three minimums and never rolled anything over, the store card alone would take about 123 months — more than ten years — and the three debts together would cost roughly $13,700 in interest. That is the number both strategies are fighting.

How the snowball plays out

The snowball sorts by balance: Visa ($2,100), then the store card ($5,400), then the loan ($8,800). Every month, each debt gets its minimum, and everything left over goes to the smallest open balance. When a debt is cleared, its minimum payment joins the pile and goes to the next target.

  • Visa cleared: month 5.
  • Store card cleared: month 16.
  • Personal loan cleared: month 24.
  • Total interest: about $2,752.

Notice what the 27% store card does while the snowball works on the Visa: it keeps compounding. That is the avalanche camp’s whole argument, and it shows up in the interest bill.

How the avalanche plays out

The avalanche sorts by rate: the store card (27%) first, then the Visa (17%), then the personal loan (11%).

  • Store card cleared: month 12.
  • Visa cleared: month 15.
  • Personal loan cleared: month 24.
  • Total interest: about $2,544.

So the avalanche saves roughly $208 here. That is real money, but it is about 1.3% of the $16,300 owed, and the finish line is identical. Why so small? Because the biggest cost in this plan is not the order — it is the $800 a month and the rollover of freed-up minimums. Once you commit to those, the order is a refinement.

When the order really matters

Change the debts to: Card A, $900 at 15% (minimum $35); Card B, $11,000 at 29% (minimum $300); Loan C, $3,000 at 8% (minimum $90), with $300 a month extra. Now the most expensive debt is also by far the biggest.

Here the avalanche saves about $1,050. But look at the last row: the avalanche user goes more than two years without closing a single account, since it dumps everything on the $11,000 card. For some people that is fine; for many it is where the plan dies.

The part the spreadsheet ignores: whether you will stick with it

Behavioral research on real consumers has found that people who close accounts early are more likely to finish the whole job; one well-known study of customers of a debt-settlement company found exactly that pattern. A visible win, such as a zero balance, reinforces the habit of paying. The cost of the snowball is, at worst, a few hundred to a couple of thousand dollars. The cost of abandoning an avalanche in month 14 is the entire plan.

A practical way to choose: compare the interest difference between the two methods on your own debts (the Debt Snowball Calculator shows the snowball version; the Debt Payoff Calculator shows each debt alone). If the gap is a few hundred dollars, pick the method you will actually follow. If it is well over a thousand and you have a record of sticking to plans, take the avalanche.

Three rules that matter more than the method

  1. Pay every minimum, every month. A missed payment brings fees and sometimes penalty rates that overwhelm any strategy gains.
  2. Keep a small cushion first. A starter emergency fund of about $1,000 stops one surprise bill from landing back on a card. The snowball calculator builds this in before extra money goes to debt.
  3. Roll freed-up payments forward. The single biggest accelerant is refusing to “spend” a minimum payment once its debt is gone.

A hybrid worth considering

Nothing says you have to pick one. A common compromise is to knock out any debt that is tiny and nearly done (a quick win) and then switch to highest-rate-first for everything else. Another is to avalanche but split the extra payment, putting most toward the top-rate debt and a token amount toward the smallest balance so an early victory still arrives.

What neither method fixes

Neither one changes the behavior that built the balances. If new charges keep landing on the cards, a payoff plan is rowing against a current. It also does not replace a conversation about whether a lower-rate consolidation, a hardship plan or a nonprofit credit counselor would serve you better; the Debt Consolidation Calculator tests the first of those. And all the figures above assume fixed rates and no new borrowing.

How to run your own comparison

  1. List every debt with its balance, APR and minimum payment.
  2. Decide how much you can send in total each month: minimums plus extra.
  3. Enter the debts in the Debt Snowball Calculator to see the smallest-first timeline.
  4. The snowball calculator only works smallest-first, so for the avalanche, step through it by hand: put all the extra money on the highest-APR debt in the Debt Payoff Calculator, and when that debt is gone, add its payment to the next one.
  5. Pick the plan, set up automatic payments, and revisit the numbers every few months.
