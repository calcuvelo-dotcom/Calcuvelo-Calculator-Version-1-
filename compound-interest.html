What is the Compound Interest Calculator?

Every savings plan eventually runs into the same question: if I put this much away now and this much every month, what will it be worth in a few years — and how much of that did I actually earn? This calculator answers it for a single account growing at a steady rate. It is meant for people comparing a savings account with an index fund, sizing a down-payment goal, or deciding whether an extra $50 a month is worth the squeeze.

It splits the ending balance into two layers, the money you deposited and the interest that piled on top, because that split is the whole story of compounding: early on, your deposits do nearly all the work; late in the game, the interest does.

How to use it, input by input

  • Starting amount. What is in the account today. Zero is a perfectly valid answer if you are starting from scratch.
  • Monthly contribution. The amount you add at the end of every month. Be honest here; a number you can sustain beats an ambitious one you abandon in month four.
  • Annual return. The big lever. A high-yield savings account might pay 3–4%, a bond fund a point or two more, and a broad stock-market index has historically averaged somewhere around 7% a year before inflation, with wild swings along the way. Avoid plugging in 10–12% because a pitch deck said so.
  • Years and months. Your horizon. Use the months box for goals like “a down payment in 4 years and 8 months.”

Switch to the table view to see each year’s opening balance, interest earned and contributions, which is handy for checking the numbers against a statement.

How does the underlying math work?

The balance is updated once a month: last month’s balance grows by one-twelfth of the annual rate, then your deposit lands.

balancen = balancen−1 × (1 + r ÷ 12) + deposit

Run that for every month and you get the closed form for the ending balance, where P is the starting amount, i is the monthly rate (annual rate ÷ 12), c the monthly deposit and n the number of months:

FV = P × (1 + i)n + c × [ (1 + i)n − 1 ] ÷ i

The first term is your starting amount compounding on its own; the second is the stream of deposits, each one compounding for however many months it has been in the account.

Worked example: $5,000 plus $300 a month for 25 years

Enter a $5,000 starting amount, $300 a month, 6% a year and 25 years. The calculator returns about $230,223. You deposited $95,000 of that ($5,000 + 300 × 300 months), so roughly $135,223 — 59% of the total — is interest.

Now keep everything the same and change only the return:

  • At 4% the account reaches about $167,808 (interest: $72,808).
  • At 8% it reaches about $322,009 (interest: $227,009).

Four percentage points either side of the middle case moves the result by roughly $62,000 down or $92,000 up, on identical deposits. That asymmetry is why the return assumption deserves more of your skepticism than the contribution amount does.

Real-world practical application

Because the tool shows the interest share explicitly, it is good at settling arguments. Is it better to put a $20,000 windfall into a savings account or an index fund? Run both rates. How much do you need to set aside each month to have $40,000 for a house in six years? Nudge the monthly box until the ending balance clears the target. Teaching a teenager why a Roth IRA or an early savings habit matters? Let them drag the years slider and watch the interest layer overtake the deposit layer.

What the result leaves out

The balance is a nominal figure: it does not subtract inflation, so $230,000 in 25 years will buy considerably less than $230,000 does today (the Inflation Calculator quantifies that). It also ignores taxes on interest, account fees, and the fact that real investments do not earn a smooth 6% — a bad year early in the plan hurts more than a bad year late. Finally, deposits are assumed to arrive at the end of each month; depositing at the start would add a little.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does it matter how often interest compounds?

A little, but less than people expect. A 6% nominal rate works out to about 6.17% a year with monthly compounding and 6.18% with daily compounding. Whether you save or borrow, the headline rate and the time horizon matter far more than the compounding schedule.

Why does growth feel so slow in the first decade?

Interest is calculated on the balance, and the balance starts small. In the worked example above, the account earns only about $400 of interest in year one but nearly $9,000 a year by year twenty. Most of the dramatic growth is back-loaded, which is why quitting early is so costly.

What return should I assume for a savings goal under five years?

Use the rate your savings account or certificate actually pays today, not a stock-market average. Money you need within a few years should not be exposed to a market drop, so the realistic return is the safe one.

How do I find the monthly amount needed to reach a target?

There is no target box, but you can solve it by trial: set the years and return, then adjust the monthly contribution until the ending balance meets your goal. Because the result moves in a straight line with the contribution, two or three guesses usually get you within a few dollars.

Is the result adjusted for inflation?

No. To see what the money is worth in today’s terms, take the ending balance to the Inflation Calculator, or use the Real ROI Calculator to convert your assumed return into an after-inflation return first.
