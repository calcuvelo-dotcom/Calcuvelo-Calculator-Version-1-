What is the High-Interest Debt Consolidation Calculator?

When a credit card balance has outgrown your monthly budget, two escape routes come up quickly: move the balance to a card that charges 0% for a while, or borrow from your own 401(k) and repay yourself. They feel similar — “borrow cheaply to kill an expensive debt” — but they cost very different things. One costs a fee and a credit-score dip; the other costs future growth and exposes you to a repayment cliff if you change jobs.

This calculator puts the measurable costs of each route side by side, so the decision rests on numbers rather than on which option sounds friendlier.

Filling it in

  • Debt, current APR, desired payoff period. The balance you want to eliminate and how many months you would like to take. The APR matters because it is what a transferred balance reverts to when the promo ends.
  • Promo window and transfer fee. Typical 0% offers run 12–21 months with a 3–5% fee on the amount moved.
  • 401(k) loan rate, term and origination fee. Plans set these; the rate is often prime plus one point, and the loan is repaid with interest into your own account. Most plans cap loans at the lesser of $50,000 or half your vested balance.
  • Expected credit-score impact. A user-entered estimate (the tool displays it, it does not calculate it) for the hard inquiry and new account.

How does the underlying math work?

Card: the fee is added to the balance, and the total is paid in equal monthly amounts across your chosen payoff period. Months inside the promo window accrue no interest; any months after it accrue interest on the remaining balance at your current APR.

401(k) loan: a standard installment payment, where L is the loan, i the monthly rate and n the term in months:

payment = L × i × (1 + i)n ÷ [ (1 + i)n − 1 ]

The origination fee is added to the total cost. The interest on a 401(k) loan goes into your own account, so in a literal sense it is not “lost”; the real cost is the investment growth the borrowed money does not earn while it is out.

Worked example: $12,000 at 24%

Take a $12,000 balance at 24% APR, a 3% transfer fee, an 18-month promo, and a 401(k) loan at 8.5% over 24 months with a 1% origination fee.

  • Card, paid off in 12 months: fee $360, payment about $1,030, interest $0, total about $12,360.
  • 401(k) loan: payment about $545, interest about $1,091 (paid to yourself), fee $120, total about $13,211.

On pure cost, the card wins by about $851 — but only because the payoff finishes inside the promo. Now stretch the card payoff to 30 months: the payment drops to about $412, but months 19–30 accrue 24% interest, adding about $744, and the card’s total rises to about $13,104. The advantage over the 401(k) loan shrinks to roughly $107, and then credit-score and job-risk factors decide it.

Real-world practical application

The tool is most useful as a reality check on the promo: can you actually afford the monthly amount that clears the balance in time? If $1,030 a month is out of reach, the 0% card is a smaller win than the ad suggests, and a longer-term fixed-rate personal loan may be a better comparison to run elsewhere. It is equally useful for the 401(k) route: the repayment is a fixed obligation that continues, often within weeks, if you leave your employer, so a plan that assumes a stable job should say so out loud.

What the numbers cannot tell you

Neither option fixes the habit that created the balance; both will feel like a clean slate, and a card can fill up again. The 401(k) route has an opportunity cost the page cannot price: if the market rises sharply while your money is out of it, you miss that growth. Interest on a 401(k) loan is not tax-deductible, and an unpaid balance after a job change is treated as a taxable distribution, plus a 10% penalty if you are under 59½. Treat the comparison as one input and talk to a nonprofit credit counselor if the balance is large.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a balance-transfer fee worth paying?

Usually, if you can clear the balance during the promo. In the example above, a $360 fee avoided over $1,000 of interest on a 24-month alternative. It stops being worth it if you cannot finish in time, because the remaining balance returns to a high APR.

Will a balance transfer hurt my credit score?

A new account and a hard inquiry typically cause a small, temporary dip, often cited as around 10–20 points, and a higher card limit can improve your utilization ratio over time. The effect varies by credit profile.

What happens to a 401(k) loan if I leave my job?

The outstanding balance usually becomes due in full by a set deadline (often your next tax filing date under current rules, though plans vary). If you cannot repay, the unpaid amount is treated as a distribution, which is taxed and may carry a penalty.

Is the interest I pay on a 401(k) loan really “paid to myself”?

Yes, it is credited to your account, but it is not extra return. The dollars you borrowed are not invested in the market while they are out, so you give up that growth, and you repay the loan with after-tax money.

Are there other options for consolidating?

Yes: a fixed-rate personal loan, a home-equity loan (which puts your house at risk), or a nonprofit debt-management plan. This calculator compares only the two options named; it does not rank every alternative.
