What is the Salary to Hourly Converter?

Job offers arrive in one unit and bills arrive in another. A recruiter quotes an annual salary; a freelance client asks for a day rate; a side gig pays by the hour; your rent is due monthly. This converter translates between all of them, in either direction, and lets you say how many hours you work and how many weeks you are actually paid for.

That last part is what separates it from the back-of-the-envelope divide-by-2,080 calculation, and it matters more than most people expect.

How to use it

  • Pick a direction. Start from an annual salary to see the hourly equivalent, or from an hourly wage to see the annual figure.
  • Hours per week and days per week. The standard full-time week is 40 hours over five days. Use your real hours if they differ.
  • Weeks per year and time off. Fifty-two weeks is the default. The time-off box removes weeks you are not working; for a salaried employee, those weeks are still paid, so the “effective hourly” rate shown is what you earn for each hour you actually put in.

Set time off to zero if you want the textbook 2,080-hour convention (40 hours × 52 weeks).

How does the underlying math work?

Everything is derived from the annual figure and the hours actually worked:

hourly = annual ÷ (hours per week × (weeks − time-off weeks))
weekly = annual ÷ 52    bi-weekly = weekly × 2    monthly = annual ÷ 12

Going the other way, annual pay is the hourly rate times hours per week times the number of weeks you are paid. Daily pay is the weekly figure divided by days worked per week.

Worked example: a $78,000 offer

Enter $78,000, 40 hours a week and 3 weeks of time off:

  • Weekly: $1,500; bi-weekly: $3,000; monthly: $6,500; daily: $300.
  • Effective hourly: $39.80 (78,000 ÷ 1,960 hours). Using the 2,080-hour convention instead gives $37.50.

Now flip it. A contractor charging $42 an hour, working 40 hours a week for 50 weeks (two weeks off, unpaid), grosses $84,000 a year, or $7,000 a month. That looks better than the $78,000 job — until you account for what the contractor must pay for and what the salaried employee gets: self-employment tax (about 15.3% in the US), health insurance, paid holidays and retirement matching. Benefits are commonly estimated at roughly 30% of total compensation, so the $42 contractor needs a considerably higher rate to match.

Real-world practical application

Freelancers use it to set a rate that hits a target income after unpaid weeks. Job seekers use it to compare a salaried offer against an hourly one on equal footing. Budgeters use the monthly and bi-weekly numbers to line up pay against rent and bills (a bi-weekly schedule means 26 paychecks a year, so two months of the year contain three). It is also a useful reality check on a side-gig idea: if the effective hourly rate comes out below what you can earn elsewhere, the gig is a bad trade.

Things this does not capture

All figures are gross, before tax and deductions, so they will not match your paycheck. Overtime, bonuses and commission are not modeled. Unpaid lunch breaks, commute time and unbillable hours (for freelancers, often 20–30% of the week) lower the true hourly return on your time; an honest effective rate counts them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is 2,080 hours used so often?

It is 40 hours × 52 weeks, the number of scheduled hours in a standard full-time year. It treats paid holidays and vacation as hours worked, so it yields the lowest hourly equivalent for a given salary.

Should vacation and holidays count as worked hours?

For comparing salaried pay with hourly pay for the same job, many people count them as paid hours (2,080). For deciding what your time is worth when you actually work, the time-off adjustment gives the more telling number.

How do I turn an hourly wage into a comparable salary?

Multiply by hours per week and by the number of weeks you are paid. Remember to subtract any unpaid time off and to account for benefits the salaried role includes.

Why do two months a year have three bi-weekly paychecks?

Bi-weekly means every two weeks, which is 26 pay periods a year, so two months contain three paychecks. Semi-monthly pay (twice a month) gives 24 equal checks.

Does this include taxes?

No. Everything is gross pay. Use it to compare offers and rates; for take-home pay, apply your own tax and deduction estimates.
