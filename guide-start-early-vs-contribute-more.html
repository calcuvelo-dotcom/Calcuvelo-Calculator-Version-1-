Start early or contribute more later? What the compounding math says

Run your own numbers: Compound Interest Calculator, Retirement / 401(k) Calculator, Rule of 72 Calculator.

One of the most repeated pieces of money advice is that starting early matters more than how much you contribute. It is usually illustrated with two savers, one who starts young and quits, and one who starts late and keeps going. The story is true as far as it goes, but it is often told in a way that hides the conditions. This guide runs the numbers carefully and reports what it finds, including the cases where “start early” does not win.

The two savers

Emma starts saving at 25. She puts $200 a month into a fund for ten years, until she is 35, and then never contributes another dollar. Liam does nothing until 35, then puts away $200 a month every month until he is 65. Assume both earn 7% a year, compounded monthly, before inflation and tax.

Money contributedBalance at 35Balance at 45Balance at 55Balance at 65
Emma (25–35)$24,000$34,617$69,568$139,809$280,968
Liam (35–65)$72,000$0$34,617$104,185$243,994

At 65, Emma has about $37,000 more than Liam, after contributing a third as much. That is the famous result. Each of Emma’s dollars turned into about $11.70; each of Liam’s into about $3.40.

Why the early dollars are worth so much

At 7%, money doubles roughly every ten years (the Rule of 72 says 72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10.3). Emma’s $34,617 at age 35 has thirty years to run, which is about three doublings: $34,617 becomes roughly $69,000, then $139,000, then $281,000. Liam’s first dollars have only thirty years, and his last dollars have only a few months. In effect, the early contributions get the full benefit of compounding, while most of Liam’s late contributions get very little of it.

What if Liam contributes more?

Here is the part the cautionary tale usually skips. Liam does not have to match Emma’s result by starting early; he can catch up by contributing more, and it takes less than you might think:

  • To match Emma’s $280,968 at 65, Liam needs to contribute about $230 a month, only 15% more than $200. He would pay in about $82,900 in total, versus Emma’s $24,000.
  • If Liam doubles to $400 a month, he reaches about $487,988, far beyond Emma’s balance.

So “start early beats contributing more later” is true per dollar, and false per outcome, if the later saver can really afford to contribute more. The honest comparison is not early versus late; it is how much each dollar costs you in the effort and sacrifice of earning it. Emma’s $24,000 is cheap because it is small; what makes her result impressive is how little she needed to put in.

The best of both

If Emma had simply kept going, contributing $200 a month from 25 to 65 (40 years, $96,000), her balance at 65 would be about $524,963. That is nearly double Emma’s result and more than double Liam’s, and it is the real lesson. Starting early and continuing is the strongest combination, and nothing in the example says to stop after ten years.

Does it depend on the rate?

It does. Rerun the same two savers at 5% instead of 7%:

  • Emma: about $138,752 at 65.
  • Liam: about $166,452 at 65.

At 5%, Liam wins. The edge of compounding depends on the growth rate being high enough that early dollars multiply faster than late contributions accumulate. At lower returns, the longer period of contributions matters more than the head start. This is why it is worth running scenarios with a cautious rate rather than a hopeful one. The Compound Interest Calculator makes that a thirty-second exercise.

What waiting costs, in dollars

Hold the contribution at $200 a month and the return at 7%, and vary only when you begin. Starting at 25 and continuing to 65 (40 years) produces about $524,963. Starting five years later, at 30, produces about $360,211. Those five missing years of contributions total only $12,000, yet the final balance is about $164,700 lower. Starting ten years later leaves about $243,994, a shortfall of about $281,000 for $24,000 of contributions skipped. Delay is cheap for the first few years and then, at the end of a long horizon, startlingly expensive.

A middle path: contributions that grow with you

If you cannot start large, a gradual increase can do a lot of the work. Compare $200 a month held flat for 30 years with $200 a month that rises 3% each year, roughly in step with raises. At 7% the flat plan ends near $243,994 on $72,000 of contributions. The rising plan ends near $333,432 on about $114,181 of contributions — about $89,000 more at the end, from a contribution that never felt like a jump because it grew slowly. Pairing automatic increases with raises is among the least painful ways to improve a result.

Inflation changes the size of the prize, not the ranking

These are nominal dollars. At 3% inflation over 40 years, prices roughly triple, so Emma’s $280,968 at 65 is worth about $86,000 in today’s purchasing power. The comparison between the savers holds, but the absolute numbers are a reminder to look at real results. The Inflation Calculator converts any future figure into today’s terms.

What to take from this

  • Time is the cheapest ingredient you have. A dollar invested at 25 does far more work than one at 45. If you can start with a small amount, do it.
  • You are not doomed if you start late. Higher contributions, a higher savings rate after a raise or a paid-off debt, and employer matches can close much of the gap.
  • Do not stop. In the example, the strongest outcome is simply early and continued saving.
  • Be honest about the rate. The ranking flips between 7% and 5%. A 7% return is an average with wide swings, not a promise.
  • Raise contributions with your income. Increasing a 401(k) contribution by a point each time you get a raise is nearly painless and compounds for decades; the Retirement / 401(k) Calculator shows how large the effect is.

How to test your own situation

  1. Open the Compound Interest Calculator and enter your current balance, what you can contribute each month, a cautious return and your years to retirement.
  2. Note the ending balance, then raise the monthly amount by $50 and note the change.
  3. Set the years back by five to see what a delay costs you in dollars.
  4. Repeat at two different returns, say 5% and 7%, and plan around the lower result.

The numbers in this guide are illustrations of a mathematical relationship, not predictions of what any investment will earn.
